Student mobility is one of the most visible successes of European cooperation, and the Erasmus programme has become a symbol of it. But who actually goes abroad? This report from Demo Mobility Alliance reviews participation in international exchange and study abroad schemes and finds that students from lower-income families, students with disabilities and vocational learners are still under-represented.

The main obstacle is money. Monthly grants rarely cover the real cost of living in expensive host cities, and payment often arrives after the student has already paid a deposit for housing. Students who cannot rely on their family to cover the gap simply do not apply. Housing is the second barrier: rents near universities have risen faster than grants, and short stays make it hard to rent on the regular market.

Information is a third problem. Students who are the first in their family to attend university often do not know that exchange is an option or how to apply. Language preparation is available mostly to those who already speak the host language.

We recommend higher and country-adjusted grants, payment of at least eighty percent of the grant before departure, a guaranteed place in host-university housing for exchange students, and dedicated mobility schemes for vocational education and training. Universities should appoint an advisor for students with fewer opportunities and report on who takes part.

International exchange should not be a privilege of those who can afford it. It should be a normal part of study for anyone who wants it.
