Universities teach professions, but do they teach citizenship? This study from Demo Campus Network reviews how higher education institutions in Europe prepare students for democratic life and finds large differences between universities, countries and disciplines.

In some institutions civic education is a compulsory module with academic credit. In many others it is an optional course taken by a small group of students who are already interested, or a series of events with no link to the curriculum. Students in technical and scientific fields are the least likely to encounter questions of democracy, ethics or public debate during their studies.

The study highlights good practice. Service learning, where students work with community organisations and reflect on the experience, strengthens both academic skills and a sense of responsibility. Debates, student parliaments and simulations of European institutions help students understand how decisions are made. Universities that include students in their governing bodies give them a practical lesson in participation.

We recommend credit-bearing civic modules available in every discipline, funding for service learning, and training for academic staff who teach citizenship. Universities should publish what they offer and report participation figures. Academic freedom and institutional autonomy must be respected, but the mission of a university includes preparing graduates for democratic life.

A graduate who can analyse a policy, listen to a different view and take part in public decisions is as valuable to society as one who has a degree in the subject of their choice.
